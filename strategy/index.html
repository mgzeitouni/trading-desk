A Monthly XSP Put Spread with a VIX Gate

Strategy v9 · October 21, 2026 · Backtests on ORATS end-of-day SPX options 2007–2026 and real XSP quotes 2019–2026, with the corrected roll cost (Oct 19). Not investment advice.

Summary

Once a month the account sells a put credit spread on XSP, the mini S&P 500 index option, about 11% below the market and 5% of the index wide, expiring 25 to 38 days out. It collects the credit, holds to the last trading day before expiry and rolls into next month’s spread in the same session. Two rules keep it out of the losses that sink most put sellers: it does not open a new spread while VIX is at or above 32, and it buys a spread back the day its cost to close reaches six times the credit. A third rule brings it back in quickly after a spike: once VIX has fallen to 75% of its 20-day high (and is below 60), it sells again into the still-rich premium. Size is the whole account divided by one spread’s maximum loss, so profits compound.

On SPX history from 2007, with whole contracts, realistic fills and the measured cost of buying back the expiring spread, a single sleeve started with $5,000 returned 18.5% a year on average (14.0% from the worst start month), with a Sortino of 2.95 and an average maximum drawdown of −22.5% (−28.1% at worst). The S&P 500 with dividends returned about 14.9% over the same windows with drawdowns of −35% to −56%. The strategy is bullish: it makes money when the index stays above a level 11% below today, and its large losses come from fast drops. The VIX gate and the stop limit those losses but do not remove them, and an overnight gap of 15–20% would cost most of the account.

1. The trade

XSP is one tenth of SPX: European-style, cash-settled, PM-settled on the third Friday, and taxed under Section 1256 (60% long-term, 40% short-term, whatever the holding period). Cash settlement means no assignment of shares; European exercise means no early assignment.

The spread sells the highest listed put at or below 89% of XSP and buys the highest listed put at or below the short strike minus 5% of XSP. At the October 19 close (XSP 769.07, VIX 18.44) that was the November 20 684 / 645 put spread. The broker holds the width less the credit as collateral, about $3,850 per spread at today’s index level, and that collateral earns Robinhood’s cash interest while it is held. The spread keeps the full credit if XSP settles above the short strike, loses linearly between the strikes, and loses the full width less the credit if XSP settles below the long strike.

2. Rules

These are the rules the bot runs, as stored in the strategy record the dashboard reads.

RuleWhat it does
GateOpen a spread only if the prior VIX close is below 32, or VIX is at or below 75% of its 20-day closing high and below 60 (post-peak re-entry).
StrikesMonthly (third-Friday) XSP expiry 25–38 days out. Sell the highest listed put at or below 89% of XSP; buy the highest listed put at or below the short strike minus 5% of XSP.
StopBuy the spread back at ~3:45pm ET when its cost to close is at least 6× the credit received. Auto-approved.
RollOn the last trading day before expiry (normally Thursday) at ~3:45pm ET, buy back the expiring spread (auto-approved) and sell next month’s spread (needs your approval).
SizeContracts = whole number of (account value less collateral held by other trades) ÷ ((width − credit) × 100), capped by the account’s contract setting. Below one spread’s collateral the account waits in cash.

3. Why these settings

VIX gate at 32. Without a gate, the backtest has −90% blow-ups: spreads sold into the second and third legs of 2008 and 2020. A cutoff of 30–32 removed them. At 35 the 2008 losses return; at 25–28 the gate sits out most of 2022, which was one of the best years for the trade.

Post-peak re-entry. Premium is richest just after a spike. Allowing entry above the cap once VIX has fallen 25% from its 20-day high improved every variant tested. Most of the strategy’s gains in crash years come from these re-entries, not from anything that pays during the fall itself.

89% short, 5% wide, 25–38 days. 87–90% short strikes all work; 89% was the best single sleeve. Wider spreads earn less per dollar of collateral but lose less in a gap, and at a $5,000 account 5% wide gave the best worst-start drawdown (−30% against −40% to −42% at 3.5–4% in the same test). Shorter expiries (14–21 days) and longer ones (35–49) both did worse, as did staggering several expiries.

Stop at 6×, no take-profit. The stop/take-profit grid on the same sleeve: 6× best, 5× and 8× close, 4× clearly worse (it stops out of trades that recover), and no stop at all gave a −72% drawdown. Every take-profit level (50%, 70%, 85% of maximum profit) reduced risk-adjusted returns, because it closes the cheapest part of the trade and reopens risk earlier.

Roll Thursday, one session. Buying back the expiring short put a day early costs about $0.02–0.03 (measured on 2023–25 XSP quotes), frees the collateral, avoids settlement on a Friday afternoon, and lets close and reopen happen in one session with one approval. Letting the spread expire and reopening Monday cost 1.5–2 points a year.

5. Account size and payouts

At small sizes one spread is a large share of the account, so whole-contract rounding costs return. Returns improve up to about $15k and then flatten; adding sleeves with different strikes and gates (88% / 89% / 90%, gate variants) trades a little return for shallower drawdowns, but only once each sleeve can hold a contract.

Six sleeves at $25k is worse than one: the sleeves cannot each hold a whole contract, and 2008 reached −35%. The bot runs one sleeve; more sleeves are a decision for $25k and above.

Taking profits out monthly

Paying out everything above the starting $5,000 at each month end (high-water style) averaged 16.7% of the base a year (12.7% from the worst start). The account sat below its base 29% of the time, at worst 28% below. The income is lumpy: crash-aftermath years paid 30–40% of the base, while 2010–2017 paid 3–10%.

7. Risks

Gap risk. The stop only works on a closing price. An overnight drop of 15–20%, like October 1987, would take most of the account, because at 100% collateral every dollar is in open spreads. This is not in the 2007–2026 sample. Size the account as money that could be mostly lost in such an event.

Ordinary drawdowns. Expect a −15% to −30% drawdown every few years, normally recovered within months. Single-sleeve history includes flat stretches of several years (2012–2017).

Direction. The trade is short a put. It does well in calm and rising markets and in the aftermath of a crash, and loses in a fast decline. It is not a hedge against an equity sell-off; the VIX gate is the only bearish rule, and it only stops new risk.

Model sensitivity. The VIX cutoff matters (30–32 good; 25–28 and 35 bad). Stop fills in a fast crash are not measured and could be worse than 35% toward the natural price. Before 2019 the test uses SPX ÷ 10 because XSP quotes were thin; on real XSP that proxy was 1–2 points a year optimistic.

Live versus backtest. About twelve trades a year is far too few to re-fit parameters on. Live data is used to check the assumptions (fills, roll cost, stop fills) and to raise alarms; the rules change only on a yearly review or an alarm, only if a new version beats this one from every start date, and only with approval.

8. How the bot runs it

Time (ET, trading days)What happens
09:40Reads VIX, XSP and the account. Checks the gate, books any expiry, and on roll day queues the buy-back. If a new spread is due, it emails an approval request.
10:45, 13:45Reconciles positions and orders with Robinhood. No trading.
15:45Checks the stop against the live cost to close and buys back if hit; runs approved orders, exits first; re-strikes any entry from the 3:45pm XSP price; records the day’s XSP put chain for later checking.
11:15, 16:15Independent watchdog checks that the bot ran, followed the rules and holds only XSP put spreads. It can halt the strategy.
23rd monthlyReport by email: fills against the 35% assumption, P&L against the expected range, rule adherence, and alarms.

Hard limits in the bot: XSP put spreads only, caps on the entry credit and exit debit per order, and an account-equity ceiling. The strategy runs only when its status is live, and halting from the dashboard rejects every pending order.

Appendix: Modeling assumptions

Data. ORATS near-end-of-day option quotes (~3:45pm ET snapshot), SPX 2007–2026 (puts 45–100% of spot, 5–80 days) and XSP 2014–2026 (used from 2019, when quotes became reliable). XSP mids are within 2–4% of SPX ÷ 10; XSP bid-ask is 2–3× wider, about $0.08 per contract at the short strike.

Timing and fills. Decisions on the prior close, orders at the next ~3:45pm snapshot. Fills 35% of the way from mid toward the natural price (0.18 measured; 0.5 tested as stress, costing about 1.5 points a year). XSP fees $0.39 per leg.

Roll cost. The expiring short put is bought back at the larger of intrinsic value and $0.025 per XSP contract, plus one leg fee; the long expires worthless. Measured 1-day-to-expiry asks at 87–92% strikes, 2023–25: mean $0.022–0.032, median $0.02.

Sizing and cash. Whole contracts at today’s contract size, re-sized at every entry; collateral = (width − credit) × 100 as the broker computes it; cash earns the year’s average 3-month T-bill rate.

Comparison. S&P 500 total return over the same start windows, approximated from price plus dividends.
